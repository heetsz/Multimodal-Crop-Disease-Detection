\begin{abstract}
Grapevine disease recognition is usually formulated as visual classification, even though disease expression is influenced by environmental conditions. This paper evaluates whether weather measurements paired with grapevine leaf images provide useful complementary evidence. We study the GVLiD dataset of 3,477 images across Black Rot, Esca, Healthy, and Leaf Blight, and attach temperature, humidity, and rainfall summaries obtained from a date--location weather-pairing pipeline. The study follows a staged evaluation: image-only and weather-only baselines, fusion-head comparisons, feature and temporal-window ablations, and repeated-seed validation. In the principal five-seed comparison, a Swin-T image--weather concatenation model obtains $83.21\%\pm1.58$ accuracy and $0.793\pm0.025$ macro-F1, compared with $82.72\%\pm1.27$ accuracy and $0.787\pm0.011$ for image-only inference. In a separate three-seed end-to-end run, multimodal concatenation reaches $85.27\%\pm0.49$ accuracy and $0.804\pm0.010$ macro-F1, versus $84.05\%\pm0.62$ and $0.793\pm0.012$ for its image-only control. Weather-only classification is weak, and shuffled, zeroed, and grid-cell controls remain competitive, so the observed gain cannot yet be attributed exclusively to physically meaningful weather variation. Dataset auditing also reveals exact duplicates, label disagreements, a single capture date, and sparse spatial coverage. The paper therefore presents a reproducible multimodal benchmark and a transparent validity analysis rather than a deployment claim.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Grapevine diseases affect yield, fruit quality, and management decisions. A photograph of a symptomatic leaf can provide an inexpensive diagnostic signal, but visual appearance varies with illumination, camera device, leaf age, background, and symptom overlap. Deep transfer-learning models have consequently become a common approach to plant disease recognition \cite{mohanty2016using,ferentinos2018deep,plantdoc,imagenet}. Their predictions, however, are based only on the pixels presented at inference time.

Environmental context is a plausible complementary modality. Temperature, humidity, and rainfall influence pathogen activity and host stress, and may help distinguish visually similar cases when they are paired with an image using reliable time and location metadata. The practical question is not whether weather is biologically relevant in general, but whether weather variables improve classification under a fixed, reproducible data split and whether the improvement survives controls for confounding.

This work studies that question using the GVLiD grapevine leaf dataset and the project's date--location weather-pairing pipeline. We compare image-only, weather-only, and multimodal models; evaluate several fusion mechanisms; and report repeated-seed results rather than relying only on one favorable run. We also treat dataset quality as part of the experiment. In particular, we document duplicate images, folder--metadata label disagreement, the single capture date, and the limited number of weather-grid points. These observations determine how strongly the reported scores can be interpreted.

The contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item a reproducible benchmark for grapevine disease classification with image and seven-day weather inputs;
    \item a comparison of concatenation, late fusion, FiLM, and gated fusion, together with weather-feature and window ablations;
    \item repeated-seed results and control experiments that test whether the multimodal gain depends on the weather values; and
    \item an explicit data-validity audit and a leakage-aware discussion of what the current results do and do not establish.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
Early plant disease systems relied on handcrafted color, texture, and shape descriptors. Large image collections and transfer learning shifted the field toward convolutional networks, with strong results reported on curated leaf datasets \cite{mohanty2016using,ferentinos2018deep,plantdoc}. The ImageNet pretraining paradigm \cite{imagenet} made transfer learning practical when labeled agricultural data are limited. Efficient backbones such as MobileNetV2 \cite{mobilenet} are attractive for resource-constrained deployment, while modern convolutional and transformer backbones provide stronger representations \cite{convnext,swin,vit}.

Multimodal learning combines representations from different sources before or during prediction \cite{baltrusaitis2019multimodal}. Simple concatenation is a useful baseline because it makes the incremental value of an auxiliary modality measurable. More structured alternatives include late prediction fusion, feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) \cite{film}, and learned gates. In agricultural applications, environmental data can encode meaningful context, but it can also act as a proxy for collection site, date, or labeling procedure. For this reason, environmental augmentation should be evaluated with modality ablations and controls rather than only with a higher accuracy claim.

\section{Dataset and Data Audit}
\subsection{Dataset composition}
The project uses GVLiD, a grapevine leaf disease dataset documented by Gayakwad \textit{et al.} \cite{gvlid}. This setting is related to established open plant-health resources such as PlantVillage \cite{plantvillage}, but the present study focuses on grapevine classes and environmental pairing. The repository contains 3,477 image files in four folder-derived classes: Black Rot (808), Esca (888), Healthy (1,109), and Leaf Blight (672). The class distribution is therefore non-uniform, which motivates macro-averaged metrics in addition to accuracy.

The metadata contains filenames, disease fields, vineyard identifiers, geographic coordinates, capture information, and device-related fields. There are 28 vineyard identifiers. All records have the same capture date, 15 June 2023, and the recorded coordinates cover a limited geographic area. Numeric weather features are obtained from the external weather-pairing stage rather than directly from the original folder structure.

\subsection{Quality audit and label policy}
The audit found 446 exact-duplicate hash groups and 1,068 duplicate files beyond the first copy. Two duplicate groups cross class labels. It also found 1,344 disagreements between the metadata disease field and the folder label. The experiments use the folder-derived label consistently, while retaining the metadata label for auditing; this is a documented experimental convention, not evidence that either source is error-free.

The generated manifest contains 2,245 training rows, 578 validation rows, and 654 test rows. Because duplicate grouping and vineyard holdout were not completed for the reported benchmark, the partition should be interpreted as the recorded split rather than as a definitive estimate of performance on unseen vineyards. A strict follow-up should deduplicate before splitting and hold out vineyard identifiers jointly with duplicate groups.

\subsection{Weather pairing}
For each image, the weather pipeline associates the available geographic and date information with numeric temperature, humidity, and rainfall features. The main experiments summarize the seven days ending at the capture date. The weather variables are obtained from NASA POWER, a commonly used source of agro-environmental data \cite{nasa_power}. For a weather window of length $k$, the input is represented as
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{w}_{k} =
\left[\operatorname{mean}(T_{k}),\operatorname{mean}(H_{k}),\operatorname{sum}(R_{k})\right],
\label{eq:weather}
\end{equation}
where $T$, $H$, and $R$ denote temperature, humidity, and rainfall. Weather features are standardized using training-partition statistics only. Since every image has the same capture date and only two weather-grid points are represented, Eq.~(\ref{eq:weather}) should be viewed as a within-dataset contextual descriptor, not as evidence of seasonal forecasting.

\section{Method}
\subsection{Experimental protocol}
Images are resized and normalized for transfer learning. The weather vector is passed through a small multilayer perceptron (MLP). The visual encoder is either MobileNetV2 for the baseline stage or Swin-T for the repeated-seed study. The same train, validation, and test manifests are used for the compared models. Checkpoints are selected from validation performance and evaluated once on the test partition. Results are reported from the repository's generated CSV artifacts.

The principal repeated-seed study uses five seeds for image-only and multimodal comparisons. A later end-to-end validation uses three seeds and trains the selected model configuration through the complete pipeline. An earlier ConvNeXt-Tiny experiment uses one seed to compare fusion heads; it is reported as an exploratory architecture study and not mixed with the repeated-seed estimates.

\subsection{Fusion models}
Let $\mathbf{z}_{I}=f_I(x)$ be the image representation and $\mathbf{z}_{W}=f_W(\mathbf{w}_k)$ the weather representation. The concatenation model predicts
\begin{equation}
\hat{\mathbf{y}} = g([\mathbf{z}_{I};\mathbf{z}_{W}]).
\label{eq:concat}
\end{equation}
Late fusion combines predictions from separate image and weather heads. FiLM conditions image features with weather-dependent scale and shift parameters. Gated fusion learns a soft contribution from the auxiliary representation:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z} = \mathbf{z}_{I}+
\sigma\!\left(W[\mathbf{z}_{I};\mathbf{z}_{W}]+b\right)\odot W_W\mathbf{z}_{W}.
\label{eq:gate}
\end{equation}
Here $\sigma$ is the element-wise sigmoid and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. The equations describe the model family used in the repository; they are included to make the comparison precise rather than to claim that one fusion operation is universally optimal.

\subsection{Metrics and controls}
We report accuracy, macro-precision, macro-recall, and macro-F1, following the distinction between class-averaged and aggregate classification measures discussed by Sokolova and Lapointe \cite{scikit_metrics}. For class set $\mathcal{C}$, macro-F1 is
\begin{equation}
F1_{\mathrm{macro}}=\frac{1}{|\mathcal{C}|}
\sum_{c\in\mathcal{C}}\frac{2P_cR_c}{P_c+R_c}.
\label{eq:macrof1}
\end{equation}
The study includes temperature-only, humidity-only, rainfall-only, temperature-plus-humidity, and all-feature ablations. It also evaluates one-, three-, seven-, and fourteen-day windows and multi-scale windows. Three controls are especially important: weather vectors set to zero, weather vectors shuffled across records, and a grid-cell one-hot vector with no weather values. If these controls match real weather, the multimodal score cannot be interpreted as evidence that the numerical weather history itself is responsible.

\subsection{Ablations and modality controls}
The five-seed ablations show that temperature alone obtains $0.8284$ accuracy and $0.7907$ macro-F1, while humidity alone and rainfall alone each obtain $0.8245$ accuracy and $0.7860$ macro-F1. Temperature plus humidity reaches $0.8312$ accuracy and $0.7931$ macro-F1. Window length has only a small effect: the three-day window reaches $0.8330$ accuracy and $0.7942$ macro-F1, compared with $0.8321$ and $0.7932$ for seven days. A multi-scale MLP reaches $0.8327$ and $0.7945$.

The controls are a warning against a causal interpretation. Shuffled weather reaches $0.8321$ accuracy and $0.7932$ macro-F1; zero weather reaches $0.8327$ and $0.7941$; and grid-cell one-hot reaches $0.8327$ and $0.7965$. These values are comparable to real weather concatenation. The result therefore establishes that an auxiliary contextual branch can help under the recorded split, but does not isolate the contribution of physically meaningful temperature, humidity, or rainfall dynamics.

\subsection{Class-wise behavior}
For the repeated-seed Swin-T comparison, the multimodal model improves Esca F1 from 0.7580 to 0.7714, Healthy F1 from 0.9806 to 0.9913, and Leaf Blight F1 from 0.5191 to 0.5324. Black Rot F1 decreases from 0.8910 to 0.8778. Thus, the macro-F1 gain is not uniform across classes: the multimodal representation helps several classes, but it does not resolve the hardest class or improve every disease category.

\section{Discussion}
The experiments support three bounded conclusions. First, image content is the primary signal: the weather-only MLP is weak, with $34.71\%$ accuracy and $0.2008$ macro-F1 in the baseline stage. Second, adding an auxiliary weather/context branch is consistently competitive with image-only inference and yields small to moderate gains in the repeated-seed and end-to-end runs. Third, the control results prevent a stronger claim that the gain is caused by the numerical weather history. Site identity, grid assignment, or other systematic structure may be sufficient to produce a similar result.

The fusion comparison also suggests that the interaction mechanism matters. The single-seed ConvNeXt experiment favors gated fusion on accuracy, whereas the repeated-seed Swin-T study uses concatenation as the stable reference. These findings should not be treated as a ranking of architectures across backbones because the experiments differ in backbone, seed count, and training stage.

The correct comparison with future work is protocol-based rather than score-based. Reported plant-disease accuracies can change substantially with duplicate handling, background composition, class balance, and the definition of the test split. This concern is consistent with broader evidence that benchmark performance can change under distribution shift \cite{dataset_shift}. The present paper makes those factors visible so that a future leakage-safe evaluation can be compared fairly.

\section{Limitations and Future Validation}
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Duplicate leakage:} exact duplicate groups cross the recorded partitions unless they are explicitly grouped. The next benchmark must split by duplicate group before model selection.
    \item \textbf{Vineyard generalization:} a vineyard-aware holdout is required to test transfer to an unseen vineyard rather than recognition of capture-specific appearance.
    \item \textbf{Temporal validity:} all images share one capture date, so the seven-day descriptor does not demonstrate seasonal or multi-year generalization.
    \item \textbf{Spatial coverage:} two weather-grid points are insufficient to characterize vineyard-scale microclimates.
    \item \textbf{Label reliability:} 1,344 folder--metadata disagreements require manual review or independent relabeling.
    \item \textbf{Statistical evidence:} three and five seeds quantify run variability but do not replace confidence intervals, paired tests, or an independent test set.
    \item \textbf{External validation:} images from another vineyard, device, date, and region are needed before field deployment or agronomic decision support.
\end{enumerate}

Future work should create a deduplicated vineyard-stratified split, collect multiple seasons, and evaluate weather pairing at finer spatial resolution. Explainability should also be added: Grad-CAM or attention rollout can test whether the visual branch attends to symptoms, while permutation importance can test whether the weather branch uses meaningful variables rather than site proxies.

\section{Conclusion}
This paper presents a weather-conditioned multimodal benchmark for grapevine leaf disease classification. Across the recorded GVLiD split, Swin-T with weather concatenation improves over the image-only control in both a five-seed comparison and a three-seed end-to-end validation. The improvement is promising but modest, and the zero, shuffled, and grid-cell controls show that the current evidence does not identify physical weather history as the unique cause.

The principal contribution is therefore methodological: multimodal plant-disease results should be reported together with repeated seeds, modality controls, and a dataset audit. After duplicate-group and vineyard-aware validation on multi-date data, the same pipeline can determine whether weather is a transferable diagnostic modality or merely a proxy for how this particular dataset was collected.
